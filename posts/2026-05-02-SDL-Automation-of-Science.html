🤖 Automation of Science

Reference video: The Automation of Science

The arrival of the AI era isn’t just transforming software engineering; biomedicine and drug discovery are experiencing the same shift. The term “autonomous laboratory” (or Self-Driving Lab, SDL) is appearing more frequently in the news.

An autonomous laboratory, also known as a self-driving lab (SDL), builds on a concept that emerged in the late 2000s. At its core is a closed-loop system (Design–Make–Test–Analyze, or DMTA):

  • AI generates hypotheses and designs experiments.
  • Automated robotics execute and test them.
  • Data are fed back to the AI for analysis and optimization.
  • The cycle repeats with the next round of improved experiments.

The pioneer of this field is Dr. Ross D. King, professor at the University of Cambridge (UK) and Chalmers University of Technology (Sweden), widely regarded as a pioneer of “Automation of Science.”

⚛️ Adam (2009)

The first autonomous laboratory in history, Adam, was developed in 2009 by Dr. King in collaboration with a research team at Aberystwyth University. The name is both a reference to the biblical Adam and a tribute to Adam Smith, the 18th-century economist who strongly advocated industrial mechanization.

Adam was primarily applied to functional genomics, using yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) as a model organism. Its goal was to automatically infer and identify genes with unknown functions (estimated at 10–15% of the yeast genome), as well as to identify genes encoding orphan enzymes.

💡 What is an orphan enzyme?
An enzyme whose function is known (i.e., the reaction it catalyzes), but whose corresponding encoding gene remains unknown (often annotated as “unknown function” or incorrectly annotated).

Adam’s task was essentially to match genes of unknown function with enzymes that lacked identified genes.

What did Adam do?

  • Automatically generated hypotheses from existing biological databases and logical models, predicting which unknown genes might encode specific orphan enzymes.
  • Produced 20 hypotheses for 13 orphan enzymes and designed experiments to test them.
  • Executed experiments, analyzed the data, and iteratively refined its predictions.

Results: Adam successfully identified and experimentally validated genes for multiple orphan enzymes. The most notable was the enzyme 2-aminoadipate:2-oxoglutarate aminotransferase (2A2OA, EC 2.6.1.39) involved in lysine biosynthesis.

⚛️ Eve (2015 → 2018)

In 2015, King collaborated with a research team at Chalmers University of Technology in Gothenburg, Sweden, to evolve Adam into Eve, designed to automate early-stage drug discovery.

The 2018 study demonstrated Eve’s capabilities in screening ~1,600 compounds from the Johns Hopkins Clinical Compound Library. By modeling structure–activity relationships, it predicted which compounds were worth testing, then designed and executed experiments to validate them.

One notable finding was that triclosan, a common antimicrobial compound used in toothpastes and soaps, can inhibit the enzyme DHFR (dihydrofolate reductase), which is essential for the survival of the malaria parasite (Plasmodium falciparum) during its dormant phase in the liver.

Eve’s workflow:

  1. In silico pre-screening: ML models rapidly predicted the most promising compounds.
  2. Experimental execution: Selected compounds are tested in cultured cells containing malaria parasites, with automated systems measuring inhibition effects.
  3. AI learning: Experimental results are fed back into the system to identify structural or physicochemical features associated with activity.
  4. Optimization: Algorithms such as Bayesian optimization predict promising untested compounds and schedule the next experiments.
  5. Iterative closed loop: The cycle repeated until potent candidates were identified.

Eve shifted the focus from pure discovery to optimization and demonstrated clear efficiency gains over traditional screening.

⚛️ Genesis (in development)

In 2019, Ross King was appointed as a Wallenberg Chair under Sweden’s WASP (Wallenberg AI, Autonomous Systems and Software Program), officially launching the third-generation AI scientist, Genesis.

The goal is a true L4-level autonomous system capable of fully automating eukaryotic systems biology research in yeast. Once complete, Genesis will autonomously initiate and run up to 1,000 experiments per day.

The evolution of AI scientists from Adam to Genesis

Summary:

  • Adam: The first SDL capable of generating and experimentally validating hypotheses.
  • Eve: Shifted from pure scientific discovery to industrial application, focusing on optimization and significantly improving efficiency in drug screening.
  • Genesis: A more compact system (about one-fifth the size of Eve), aiming to autonomously generate hypotheses, design experiments, execute them, and analyze results to build systems biology models of interactions among genes, proteins, and small molecules.

King’s broader vision is to demonstrate that, in specific domains, AI scientists can conduct research faster and more cost-effectively than humans.

Dr. Ross King’s website: https://www.automationof.science/home

⚙️ Self-Driving Labs (SDLs)

In the previous section, we mentioned that Ross King’s team aims to develop Genesis into an L4 autonomous laboratory. But what exactly does L4 mean?

In 2020, Beal et al. proposed a classification framework for SDLs in Molecular Systems Biology, defining six levels of autonomy (L0–L5). At L0, laboratories are entirely human-operated, while higher levels progressively shift the balance from human intelligence to artificial intelligence.

In 2026, Jason Kelly, co-founder and CEO of Ginkgo Bioworks, introduced an industry-oriented framework called LoLA (Levels of Laboratory Autonomy).

Levels of Laboratory Autonomy (LoLA)

Today, several systems are considered to have reached at least L3 autonomy, including Ginkgo’s Nebula + GPT-5, A-Lab, Genesis, and LabGenius EVA.

  • Beal et al. (2020) → Levels of autonomy in synthetic biology engineering
  • Kelly’s LoLA framework → discussed in his 2026 talks and Ginkgo announcements.

🌐 Berkeley Lab: A-Lab

The A-Lab is an autonomous laboratory platform developed at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, operational since 2023. It is designed to accelerate the synthesis and discovery of inorganic materials.

A-Lab forms a complete closed-loop system by integrating:

  • AI planning layer: Selects candidate materials predicted to be stable using datasets such as the Materials Project and DeepMind outputs, and learns synthesis routes from literature. The AI can propose up to 5 synthesis pathways per target and continuously optimizes them via active learning.
  • Robotic execution layer: Includes 3 automated workstations for handling powder dosing, high-temperature synthesis, and product characterization.
  • Data analysis & iteration: AI interprets experimental results in real time, determines target yield, and decides on the next experiment.

This system operates with minimal human intervention and represents the first fully autonomous SDL for solid-state synthesis using inorganic powders.

Published in Nature in 2023, the group demonstrated A-Lab’s ability to close the gap between computational prediction and experimental realization. Its integrated AI–robotics loop can operate 24/7, completing the full cycle from design to optimization, and is considered a benchmark for achieving 10–100× acceleration in materials discovery.

Key results:

  • Over 17 days of continuous autonomous operation, A-Lab performed 353 experiments (~21 per day).
  • Successfully synthesized 36 out of 57 target compounds (63% success rate), spanning diverse oxides, phosphates, and other inorganic crystals.

🌐 University of Toronto: Acceleration Consortium

Led by chemistry professor Alán Aspuru-Guzik, the Acceleration Consortium (founded 2021) is one of the world’s leading centres for SDL research and application.

Traditional materials development often takes 20+ years and costs >$100 million. AC aims to reduce this to ~1 year and ~$1 million using self-driving labs, accelerating the development of sustainable materials and molecules, including drugs, biodegradable plastics, and clean energy technologies.

AC currently operates 6 SDLs at the University of Toronto (SDL1–6) and collaborates with UBC in Vancouver on SDL7, while also connecting with more than 30 SDLs worldwide.

SDL specialties:

  • SDL1: Inorganic materials
  • SDL2: Organic small molecules
  • SDL3: Medicinal chemistry - for chemical probe development and drug discovery
  • SDL4: Polymers
  • SDL5: Formulations - materials comprising combinatorial mixing; characterization of structure and function, and application testing across personal care, healthcare, and more
  • SDL6: Human organ mimicry - next-generation human tissue models and novel therapeutics
  • SDL7: Scale-up - bridging molecular and materials discovery with scale-up and translation

Together, these SDLs form an end-to-end closed loop, from molecular design to synthesis, with SDL7 enabling scale-up, shortening the path from early discovery to real-world deployment.

Beyond academia, AC actively commercializes SDL technologies through startups and industry partnerships.

Industry collaborations:

  • BASF (May 2025): Joint SDLs for sustainable materials in agriculture, pharmaceuticals, and coatings.
  • Unilever (Sept 2025): Collaborating on SDLs for optimizing submicron emulsions in personal-care products (shower gels, lotions, etc.).
  • Bristol Myers Squibb: Developing autonomous liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) platforms for chemical purification.

AC also collaborates with Vancouver-based startup Telescope Innovations. Several commercial SDLs have already been deployed to Pfizer and the Korean Pharmaceutical and Biopharmaceutical Manufacturers Association (KPBMA).

🌐 University of Toronto: LUMI-lab

LUMI-lab is another fully closed-loop DMTA autonomous platform developed by a U of T team. It focuses on designing ionizable lipids and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) for mRNA delivery. The system trains on over 28 million 3D molecular structures and continuously improves through iterative closed-loop learning:

Model prediction → LNP synthesis & formulation → high-throughput in vitro screening → feedback and model optimization

Its three-stage training pipeline includes:

  • Self-supervised molecular pretraining: Using 13 million molecular structures with 3D conformations
  • Continual pretraining with lipids: Further training on 15 million lipid molecules
  • Closed-loop active learning: Iterative optimization across 10 experimental cycles

In their 2026 Cell publication, LUMI-lab evaluated 1,781 LNPs after 10 iterations. More than 50% of candidates showed high transfection efficiency. Notably, one candidate (LUMI-6) outperformed Moderna’s SM-102 LNP in mouse models.

In CRISPR-Cas9 lung gene editing experiments, LUMI-6 achieved a 20.3% editing efficiency in lung epithelial cells.

These results demonstrate that even with minimal initial data, closed-loop active learning and optimization can accelerate next-generation RNA delivery development.

🌐 Duke University: SAMPLE Platform

The SAMPLE (Self-driving Autonomous Machines for Protein Landscape Exploration) platform, developed by the Romero Lab (originally at the University of Wisconsin, now at Duke University), is an AI-driven system for protein engineering.

It autonomously learns relationships between protein sequences and function, designs new variants, and integrates automated experimentation and analysis into iterative cycles:
Design → Test → Learn (~9–10 hours per cycle)

In their 2024 Nature Chemical Engineering study, SAMPLE was integrated with Strateos Cloud Lab infrastructure to form a full SDL, significantly accelerating protein optimization.

Compared to traditional manual workflows:

Metric SAMPLE Platform Human Researchers (estimated)
Time to complete 20 cycles 1–2 weeks (continuous operation) Planned: ~2 months
Actual: ~6–12 months
Cost ~$5,200 (20 cycles) High (labour + overhead)
Data quality Highly reproducible, full metadata tracking More variable, less reproducible

🏦 Government Investment

SDLs are already being deployed across multiple domains, and governments worldwide are investing heavily in their development.

  • United States: The Department of Energy (DOE) supports autonomous labs in energy and materials research (e.g., PNNL). In April, ARPA-E announced $34 million in funding for 12 projects combining AI with autonomous labs.
  • Canada: The University of Toronto’s Acceleration Consortium received CAD $200 million (~USD $146 million) from the Canada First Research Excellence Fund (CFREF) in 2023. This is the largest single grant ever awarded to a Canadian university.
  • United Kingdom: ARIA launched its AI Scientist program in early 2026, investing £6 million across 12 projects. A team at King’s College London is one of them, receiving £500,000 to develop SDLs for converting agro-food by-products into high-quality protein.
  • South Korea: SDLs have been designated a national strategic priority, with a goal of deploying 500 autonomous labs by 2030. The KPBMA (Korea Pharmaceutical and Bio-Pharma Manufacturers Association) has already installed the country’s first SDL for drug development as a national AI-driven R&D training centre.
