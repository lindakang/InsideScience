Autonomous laboratories currently fall into two broad business models: physical self-driving labs (SDLs) and cloud labs.

Physical SDLs are systems such as Adam, Eve, and Genesis. These are typically enterprise-grade platforms used by large pharmaceutical or materials companies. In many cases, SDL vendors build and deploy customized autonomous laboratory systems directly for clients, such as the collaboration between Telescope Innovations and Pfizer.

By contrast, cloud labs operate under a Lab-as-a-Service (LaaS) model. A cloud lab is a highly automated physical laboratory that researchers can remotely access and control over the internet. This model is generally more suitable for startups and small-to-mid-sized biotech companies because users do not need to build or maintain their own laboratories or operate instruments themselves. Instead, experiments are executed 24/7 by robotic and automation systems managed by the cloud lab provider. Examples include Emerald Cloud Lab and Ginkgo Bioworks.

A simple way to think about the distinction is:

  • AI model = brain
  • Cloud Lab = hands
  • SDL = AI model (brain) + Cloud Lab (hands)

▫️ Cloud Lab: Lab-as-a-Service (LaaS)

The service being sold is laboratory infrastructure and remote experimental execution capability.

Scientists design or order experiments through the cloud lab platform, and the lab executes the workflows and returns the resulting data. However, the users still determine what experiments to run.

Business model

Usage-based or subscription-based pricing focused on convenience, scalability, and throughput. This model is particularly attractive for startups with small teams and limited laboratory space because they do not need to build or maintain their own wet labs.

▫️ SDL: AI Scientist-as-a-Service

The service being sold is an autonomous laboratory system composed of AI models and physical wet-lab infrastructure.

Scientists define a research objective, after which the AI autonomously generates hypotheses, designs experiments, instructs robotic systems to execute them, analyzes the results, and determines the next iteration within a closed loop.

Business model

Enterprise-scale solutions focused on accelerating R&D and generating new scientific knowledge. Large pharmaceutical companies may build proprietary SDLs through direct procurement or establish multi-year partnerships with AI companies.

2026 Market Trends

Cloud labs are becoming increasingly popular because they significantly lower the barrier to entry for teams that do not want to build and operate their own laboratories.

SDLs represent a higher-value enterprise model and are currently adopted primarily by large pharmaceutical and materials companies. For example, Telescope Innovations has deployed SDL systems for Pfizer and the Korea Pharmaceutical and Bio-Pharma Manufacturers Association, while LabGenius Therapeutics has signed multiple long-term partnerships with Sanofi.

The two models are beginning to converge. Many next-generation SDL platforms increasingly use cloud labs as the physical execution layer, creating hybrid systems that combine AI co-scientists with cloud-based robotic experimentation.

🌐 Telescope Innovations (UBC Spin-off)

Telescope Innovations is a Canadian spin-off from the University of British Columbia (UBC) Department of Chemistry. Its co-founder and CTO is Jason Hein, a professor in UBC’s Department of Chemistry and a scientific advisor to the Acceleration Consortium.

Telescope specializes in developing fully autonomous chemical synthesis and optimization platforms. Its core products include Self-Driving Labs (SDLs) and the DirectInject-LC™ platform. The company’s SDLs operate 24/7, autonomously planning, executing, analyzing, and continuously optimizing experiments, making them significantly more efficient than traditional manual workflows. Telescope focuses on applications in pharmaceuticals, specialty chemicals, and high-value materials, helping clients accelerate chemical process development, reduce R&D and manufacturing costs, and rapidly scale workflows from laboratory discovery to commercial production.

Core Technologies

  • DirectInject-LC™: A fully automated real-time reaction sampling system that directly samples chemical reactions in real time, quenches and dilutes aliquots, and injects them into an HPLC system for immediate analysis. The platform is globally distributed by METTLER TOLEDO and serves as a key enabling technology for process optimization and Process Analytical Technology (PAT).
  • SDL Platform: Telescope’s SDLs integrate automated laboratory systems, real-time analytical instruments, and AI-driven decision-making software. These systems are primarily used for chemical synthesis optimization, crystallization, and materials development. The platforms are modular and scalable, helping address bottlenecks between early-stage discovery and large-scale manufacturing.

Industry Collaborations

Since 2024, Telescope has been collaborating strategically with Pfizer to co-develop SDL technologies. The first SDL was installed at Pfizer in 2025, followed by a second, more advanced system in January 2026. The collaboration focuses on integrating automated laboratories and AI to accelerate drug development, synthesis optimization, and process scale-up.

In 2025, Telescope also partnered with the Korea Pharmaceutical and Bio-Pharma Manufacturers Association (KPBMA) and completed the installation of Korea’s first pharmaceutical Self-Driving Lab on December 9, 2025. The facility serves as the country’s first AI-driven pharmaceutical R&D training centre and is intended to promote broader SDL adoption across the Korean pharmaceutical industry.

🌐 Trilobio

Trilobio was founded in 2021 and raised $8 million in seed funding in 2025, with the goal of building Self-Driving Labs (SDLs) specifically for biology laboratories.

Its primary products are Trilobio OS and Trilobot One. Trilobio OS handles experiment design, orchestration, and result analysis, while Trilobot executes the physical workflows. Together, they form a fully closed-loop SDL platform.

Platform Components

  • Trilobio OS: A software layer that integrates AI and laboratory robotics into a closed-loop SDL system. Through a centralized interface, users can design experiments, monitor workflows in real time, and use AI-driven optimization throughout the experimental cycle.
  • Trilobot One: A compact benchtop automation robot that supports up to 8 interchangeable lab tools, such as pipettes, grippers, and tube openers, along with 16 deck positions for consumables including tip boxes and 96-well plates. Conceptually, it resembles a simplified version of the Beckman Coulter Life Sciences Biomek platform.

Following the official launch of Trilobot One and Trilobio OS in October 2025, the company opened pre-orders and demonstrations, and multiple laboratories in the United States have reportedly adopted the platform.

The company’s business model appears to focus on selling compact, biology-focused SDL systems to academic laboratories and smaller biotech companies. Its positioning emphasizes accessibility, benchtop-scale deployment, and integrated AI-driven automation for labs that may not have the resources or space for larger industrial automation platforms.

One current limitation of the Trilobot architecture is throughput and deck capacity. Supporting 8 tools and 16 deck positions may constrain workflows to relatively straightforward applications, such as cloning or basic screening assays. More complex workflows could require multiple units, which may reduce some of the advantages of its compact footprint.

From a competitive standpoint, Trilobio’s differentiation likely lies in its small form factor and integrated AI workflow layer. Established automation vendors such as Beckman Coulter Life Sciences already offer mature robotic systems like Biomek, and integrating closed-loop AI orchestration into those platforms could potentially create more scalable SDL solutions with broader experimental capabilities.

☁️ Ginkgo Bioworks: Ginkgo Cloud Lab

Ginkgo Bioworks is a U.S.-based synthetic biology company whose service offerings were once extremely broad, spanning genetically engineered microorganisms for agriculture, food flavours and fragrances, industrial enzymes, and medical applications such as gene therapy, cell therapy, and biologics. The company also developed laboratory automation and instrumentation solutions.

At its peak, Ginkgo reached a private valuation of approximately $4.2 billion in 2019, making it one of the world’s largest privately held biotechnology companies at the time. However, following its SPAC listing in 2021, the company faced significant financial pressure and underwent a major restructuring.

Since then, Ginkgo has scaled back many of its previous business lines and appears to be focusing primarily on laboratory automation and autonomous laboratory infrastructure. Based on the launch of Ginkgo Cloud Lab and its collaboration with OpenAI, the company is clearly positioning itself around autonomous laboratories. The restructuring and strategic pivot also appear to be gaining traction. Although Q1 2026 revenue came in below expectations, significantly under analysts’ estimates of $41 million, the company maintains a relatively strong cash position and continues advancing its restructuring efforts while doubling down on autonomous lab infrastructure. It remains a high-risk transformation story, but there are visible signs of operational stabilization and strategic focus.

GPT-5 (OpenAI) + Nebula (Ginkgo Bioworks)

OpenAI and Ginkgo Bioworks collaborated on an autonomous laboratory platform that combines GPT-5 with Ginkgo’s Nebula automation infrastructure. OpenAI’s GPT-5 is responsible for interpreting experimental results, designing experiments, and generating lab documentation, while Ginkgo provides the RAC (Reconfigurable Automation Cart) system to physically execute workflows.

Conceptually, GPT-5 functions as the “brain,” while RAC serves as the “hands” performing the experiments.

In a bioRxiv preprint published in February 2026, the autonomous laboratory system referenced a cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) study published by Northwestern University PhD student Meagan Olsen and used iterative optimization to redesign and improve the workflow conditions.

Olsen’s original work reportedly took 4 months and more than 40 experiments, testing 1,231 CFPS formulation combinations and reducing production costs to approximately $698 per gram. Using iterative closed-loop optimization, the GPT-5 + RAC system further reduced protein production costs to approximately $422 per gram. Relative to the previous benchmark achieved by Olsen’s team, the autonomous system reportedly achieved a 40% reduction in cost alongside a 27% increase in protein yield (g/L).

Business Model: Menu-Style (LaaS)

In March 2026, Ginkgo officially launched the Ginkgo Cloud Lab platform, integrated with its Nebula autonomous laboratory infrastructure. The Nebula facility in Boston has already deployed more than 50 RAC units, with plans to expand beyond 100 units by the end of 2026.

The platform follows a Lab-as-a-Service (LaaS) model. The website offers a catalog of ready-to-run experimental protocols, each with predefined workflows and conditions. Researchers can select a protocol, submit samples or sequences, and the robotic systems execute the workflow automatically before returning the resulting data.

One example protocol covers cell-free protein expression, purification, and quantification. After users provide DNA sequences, the Nebula system performs protein expression in 96-well plates, followed by magnetic bead purification and quantification. The workflow is positioned as a rapid screening and optimization step before larger-scale production. Pricing is currently listed at $155 per protein, while optimization services for low-expression or difficult proteins are priced at $199 per protein.

The platform also provides EstiMate, an AI tool designed to evaluate project feasibility and generate experimental quotes.

In addition to cloud-based services, Ginkgo also sells RAC hardware and supporting software, allowing customers to build customized autonomous laboratory systems on-site.

☁️ Emerald Cloud Lab

Emerald Cloud Lab (ECL) is one of the earliest large-scale commercial cloud laboratory platforms. The company originated from Emerald Therapeutics, founded in 2010 by D.J. Kleinbaum and Brian Frezza, both alumni of Carnegie Mellon University (CMU). The founders developed centralized laboratory management software, described as a laboratory operating system, and in 2014 spun the technology out of Emerald Therapeutics to launch the Emerald Cloud Lab service. ECL became an independent company in 2016 and has since raised approximately $152 million in total funding.

The ECL platform is a highly automated physical laboratory equipped with more than 200 types of instruments, including HPLC, mass spectrometers, NMR instruments, PCR machines, flow cytometers, ELISA platforms, and many others. Researchers remotely design experiments through the ECL Command Center®, submit samples, and the robotic systems automatically execute all experimental steps, including sample preparation, instrument operation, data collection, and analysis, before returning the results to the user’s database.

Business Model: Subscription-Based Cloud Lab

Unlike Ginkgo Cloud Lab, which offers many predefined protocols, ECL provides a more flexible laboratory operating environment. Users configure experimental workflows themselves by defining protocols and writing commands. In some ways, it resembles a remote-access version of systems such as Beckman Coulter Life Sciences automation platforms.

Within the ECL Command Center®, users select instruments and configure workflows based on their experimental needs. A wide range of instruments is available. For example, protein purification workflows may include sample preparation systems, incubators, FPLC systems, Western blot instrumentation, and related analytical tools. As a result, the learning curve for ECL is slightly higher than that of Ginkgo Cloud Lab.

Because ECL does not provide simple menu-style protocol selection, it operates under a subscription-based model. The company offers different plans, including academic packages and enterprise subscriptions, although pricing is not publicly disclosed and must be discussed with its sales team. Once subscribed, users can remotely access and operate the laboratory infrastructure.

ECL also maintains a close relationship with CMU. In 2021, the two organizations partnered to launch the CMU Cloud Lab initiative, positioning it as one of the first university-scale cloud laboratory environments.

Coscientist + ECL

In 2023, Dr. Gabe Gomes’ research team at CMU demonstrated its AI system, Coscientist, performing chemical experiments on the ECL platform. This became a landmark example of a commercial cloud lab being used for AI-driven autonomous science. The work was later published in Nature in December 2023.

Coscientist was an AI agent system powered by large language models, including OpenAI’s GPT-4 and Anthropic’s Claude. The system autonomously designed and executed chemistry experiments through Emerald Cloud Lab’s infrastructure.

Key features of Coscientist:

  • Natural language instructions: Users only need to provide a simple natural language instruction (for example, “identify compounds with specific properties”), after which Coscientist handles the remaining workflow autonomously.
  • Fully automated workflow: The AI agent automatically searches the literature, integrates information, selects experimental methods, converts experimental designs into machine-readable API instructions, and delegates execution to Emerald Cloud Lab.

In the study, the research team provided a simple instruction: “Perform multiple Suzuki reactions”, along with information about the available reagents.

Coscientist then autonomously completed the following workflow using ECL infrastructure:

  1. Searched the web for relevant literature to understand the principles, stoichiometry, and conditions of Suzuki-Miyaura and Sonogashira cross-coupling reactions.
  2. Selected appropriate reactants and reagents based on chemical reasoning.
  3. Generated Python code to calculate reagent quantities and control the liquid-handling robot (Opentrons OT-2).
  4. Executed the reactions using cloud laboratory robotics, including reagent mixing and heating.
  5. Analyzed reaction products using GC-MS to confirm successful synthesis of the target compounds.
  6. Iteratively optimized reaction conditions based on experimental results to improve yield.

As a result, Coscientist not only rapidly designed valid experimental procedures but also successfully synthesized the target compounds. The study demonstrated strong capabilities in self-learning, information integration, experimental planning, selection of optimal methods, and autonomous optimization, functioning much like an autonomous scientist.

This represented one of the earliest demonstrations of an AI system operating as an autonomous scientific agent on a real-world laboratory platform.

Although the Coscientist + ECL workflow has not yet become a commercial product, it strongly suggests the direction the industry may be heading.

☁️ Lila Sciences: AI Science Factory (AISF)

Lila Sciences is an AI science company founded by Flagship Pioneering in 2023 and headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The company’s stated goal is to build “Scientific Superintelligence™” through a combination of AI models and autonomous laboratory systems.

In October 2025, the company completed its Series A extension, bringing total funding to approximately $550 million. NVIDIA, through NVentures, is one of its investors.

Lila aims to create a fully closed-loop autonomous research system by combining its AI model, LILA Iris™, with proprietary robotic laboratories called AI Science Factory™ (AISF).

LILA Iris™ autonomously generates hypotheses, designs experiments, orchestrates experimental workflows, analyzes results, and iteratively optimizes subsequent experiments. AI Science Factory™ serves as the physical execution layer that carries out the experiments. Based on the company’s current positioning, the platform appears to operate at approximately Level 4+ self-driving lab capability and is progressing toward full Level 5 autonomy.

Once fully operational, AISF is expected to support a broad range of applications, including drug development (antibodies, mRNA, and cell therapies), materials discovery, clean energy technologies (such as hydrogen catalysts and carbon capture), defense and aerospace, petrochemicals, and related fields.

Business Model

Lila’s commercial strategy appears closer to a next-generation Lab-as-a-Service (LaaS) model.

Users will be able to design experiments and access results through LILA Iris™. The key differentiator is that Lila aims to provide fully autonomous closed-loop optimization, whereas platforms such as Ginkgo Cloud Lab and Emerald Cloud Lab currently focus more on user-directed automated execution.

Pricing has not yet been publicly disclosed, and current access appears to be limited primarily to invitation-based collaborations and custom partnerships. The company’s primary Cambridge facility is expected to become operational in Q3 2026.

💬 Personal Thoughts: Could Biotech Become Fully Outsourced?

Personally, I think the Lab-as-a-Service (LaaS) model is becoming a major trend, similar to how AI is reshaping the broader tech industry today.

Large companies are still reducing headcount and operational costs, while at the same time, increasingly small teams can now build companies with just a few people by outsourcing large portions of the workload to AI systems. Humans are increasingly focused on high-level decision-making rather than execution.

Recently, I heard Betty Wu (Taiwanese writer, 吳淡如) discuss the “amoeba management” model, which emphasizes operating a company with minimal internal staffing while outsourcing as much as possible. The concept originates from the Amoeba Management philosophy developed by Kazuo Inamori (稻盛和夫), former chairman and CEO of Japan Airlines. His management philosophy divides a company into many small, semi-independent business units responsible for their own profit and loss, essentially operating like multiple small companies within a larger organization.

At the moment, the biotech industry is not yet at that stage. Unlike many other industries where AI can rapidly replace large portions of the workforce, a significant amount of wet-lab work and experimental know-how still cannot be fully delegated to AI systems. However, if self-driving laboratories (SDLs) eventually reach something close to Level 4 autonomy, this model may become increasingly feasible.

After looking into platforms like Emerald Cloud Lab, I realized that many workflows I previously assumed required manual execution can already be automated, including SDS-PAGE and Western blot workflows. Honestly, I had underestimated how far laboratory automation has already progressed.

I was also reminded of a conversation with a friend working in molecular docking. He mentioned that after the computational design stage, their wet-lab screening is largely outsourced. Later, someone from an assay services company contacted me on LinkedIn and mentioned that many large pharmaceutical companies already outsource substantial portions of their experimental workflows to specialized service providers.

That made me realize that, in principle, almost the entire antibody discovery and development pipeline could potentially be outsourced.

I asked both Gemini and Perplexity whether such a model was actually feasible. Both responded that it is possible and pointed to the concept of a “virtual biotech” company. One frequently cited example is Nimbus Therapeutics, which operates with a relatively lean internal structure while outsourcing substantial portions of its R&D workflow.

Thinking about it now, if SDL-based LaaS platforms become sufficiently mature, the only major components that may still require dedicated external infrastructure could be animal studies and clinical trials.

At that point, the main question may no longer be technical feasibility, but economics: how much companies like Lila Sciences or future autonomous lab providers will charge for fully integrated AI-driven R&D infrastructure. It also raises an interesting question: what is the minimum viable cost structure for operating a biotech company under an Amoeba Management model?
