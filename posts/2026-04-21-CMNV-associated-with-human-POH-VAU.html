Recently, news regarding a new virus in China has been circulating on Threads among Taiwanese users, warning people not to eat raw seafood. They also mentioned a study published in Nature Microbiology in March reporting that Covert Mortality Nodavirus (CMNV) can cause blindness in humans. Since my background is in virology, I decided to do some research on it.

Most of the studies I found were published by Chinese researchers, including this latest one, likely because outbreaks primarily occur along the Chinese coast.

CMNV is a novel virus that has emerged in recent years. It caused massive losses in the Chinese farmed shrimp and prawn industry in 2009 and was later found to be associated with covert mortality disease (CMD). In 2014, the Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences isolated CMNV from whiteleg shrimp (Penaeus vannamei) and published the first report describing the virus. Subsequently, it was shown to infect various cultured crustaceans, including Chinese white shrimp (Penaeus chinensis), Kuruma prawn (Penaeus japonicus), giant tiger prawn (Penaeus monodon), and giant river prawn (Macrobrachium rosenbergii), distributed across coastal provinces in China.

In addition, CMNV has been detected in other invertebrates, including hermit crabs (Diogenes edwardsii), ghost crabs (Ocypode cordimanus), oysters (Crassostrea gigas), and sea cucumber (Apostichopus japonicus). In 2018, CMNV was detected in a common marine fish, the gobiid fish (Mugilogobius abei), in shrimp farming ponds, indicating cross-species transmission between invertebrates and vertebrates.

Viral Classification

Family: Nodaviridae | Genus: Alphanodavirus | Species: Covert mortality nodavirus

The family name Nodaviridae derives from Nodamura (Noda Village, 野田村) in Japan, where the first virus in this family, Nodamura virus, was isolated from Culex tritaeniorhynchus mosquitoes. The Nodaviridae family consists of two genera: Alphanodavirus and Betanodavirus. Alphanodavirus is generally considered to primarily infect insects, while Betanodavirus infects fish.

According to the ICTV, CMNV has not yet been officially classified, but current studies suggest it is more closely related to Alphanodavirus. A 2018 study confirmed that CMNV can naturally infect fish, challenging the traditional view that the host range of this genus is restricted to insects.

Virion Structure & Genome

CMNV Virion Structure and Genomic Organization

CMNV is a non-enveloped, ssRNA(+) virus with a diameter of ~25–30 nm and T=3 icosahedral symmetry. It is a small virus with a compact genome encoding 4 viral proteins. The genome consists of two segments:

  • RNA1 (Non-structural, ~3.1–3.2 kb): Encodes RdRp (Protein A) and produces a subgenomic RNA3 (sgRNA3), which encodes Proteins B1 and B2.
  • RNA2 (Structural, ~1.2–1.4 kb): Encodes the capsid protein.
Viral Protein Function / Characteristics
RdRp (Protein A) Responsible for viral RNA replication.
Protein B1 Function is currently unknown.
Protein B2 Suppresses host RNA interference (RNAi) defense mechanisms, protecting viral RNA from degradation.
Capsid Protein The precursor (protein α) is cleaved into protein β and protein γ during maturation; protein β forms the icosahedral capsid.

Prevalence and Regions

CMNV is prevalent in the coastal provinces of China. Positive rates were reported at 25.78% in crustacean samples in 2023 and 29.61% in 2024 (Zhang et al., 2026, SSRN).

  • Global Infection Rates: Wild samples from Antarctica show the highest reported positivity rate (35.5%), followed by Africa (20.4%), the Americas (16.4%), and Asia (16.4%).
  • Farmed vs. Wild: Positivity is higher in wild aquatic animals (32.8%) than in farmed animals (27.7%), suggesting environmental persistence.
  • Market Prevalence: High detection rates in crabs (61.5%), mollusks (58.7%), fish (54.7%), and shrimp (28.1%) in Chinese markets; the virus is present in edible tissues.

Symptoms Across Hosts

Crustaceans/Shrimp: Causes VCMD (viral covert mortality disease), also known as running mortality syndrome (RMS). Early detection is difficult. Symptoms include reduced activity, sinking behavior, stunted growth, softened shells, hepatopancreatic atrophy, empty gastrointestinal tracts, muscle whitening, myonecrosis, and ongoing mortality (up to 80% cumulative). Vacuolation is observed in multiple organs, including nervous tissue and retina, reflecting CMNV neurotropism.

Fish: Causes slow growth and anorexia. CNS involvement leads to abnormal swimming behavior, brain vacuolation, retinopathy, and myonecrosis.

Humans (POH-VAU): In recent reports, CMNV has been associated with ocular disease. The median incubation period is approximately 6 months. The primary manifestation is persistent ocular hypertension (POH) with recurrent anterior uveitis (VAU). Clinical features include elevated intraocular pressure, anterior chamber inflammation, keratic precipitates, and aqueous cells. Recurrent episodes may result in optic nerve damage, corneal endothelial loss, iris atrophy, and vision impairment. Higher viral load has been reported to correlate with increased intraocular pressure.

Viability and Transmission

High detection rates of CMNV RNA have been found in both frozen and fresh seafood products, indicating that viral genetic material can persist under freezing and standard market circulation conditions. However, the detection of viral RNA does not necessarily confirm the presence of infectious virus.

Transmission Routes:

  • Between aquatic animals: Primarily transmitted through cohabitation, cannibalism, or the consumption of virus-carrying live or frozen bait.
  • Human exposure: Handling seafood without protective measures has been reported in association with suspected cases, particularly involving puncture wounds or cuts from the appendages of fish and shrimp. Consumption of raw seafood or frequent contact with aquaculture environments has also been identified as a potential exposure route.

High-Risk Groups and Behaviors:

  • High-risk groups: Among reported POH-VAU cases, the highest proportion involves individuals handling seafood at home (51.4%), followed by those who consumed raw aquatic products (15.7%) and close contacts of high-risk individuals.
  • High-risk exposure modes: Unprotected aquatic animal processing, particularly involving frequent puncture wounds from the appendages of live or frozen fish and shrimp, has been reported in association with suspected cases (54.3%). Repeated exposure has been suggested to correlate with shorter incubation periods and more severe clinical presentation.

Infection Mechanisms

CMNV infects a broad range of tissues in aquatic species, including nervous tissue, the ocular retina and iris, muscle, and the hepatopancreas. Similar tissue associations have been proposed in reported human cases.

  • Neurotropism: The virus preferentially targets nervous and visual tissues, including ommatidia in shrimp and retinal structures in infected aquatic species.
  • Viral Replication: CMNV replicates extensively in the cytoplasm, leading to cytoplasmic vacuolation and karyopyknosis (condensation and shrinkage of chromatin within a cell nucleus), ultimately resulting in lytic necrosis.
  • Muscle and Hepatopancreas: In shrimp, infection causes necrosis of hepatopancreatic epithelial cells and muscle degeneration (myonecrosis).
  • Growth Interference: Infection is associated with the downregulation of host genes involved in digestion, nutrient absorption, and growth regulation.

POH-VAU Treatments

In reported human POH-VAU cases associated with suspected CMNV exposure, treatments include:

  • Pharmacological Control: After administration of ganciclovir antiviral eye drops, steroids, and intraocular pressure-lowering medications, ~70% of patients experienced reduced intraocular pressure and relief from inflammation. However, over one-third required long-term medication, and ~30% experienced loss of intraocular pressure control.
  • Glaucoma Surgery: The success rate of trabeculectomy (controlling IOP without medication) is 68.8%, while the qualified success rate (controlling IOP with or without medication) is as high as 81.3%. Although 62.5% of patients experience a recurrence of ocular inflammation after surgery, over 70% of these cases do not show a subsequent rise in IOP and can be managed with short-term treatment.
